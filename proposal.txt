Neural mechanisms of sensorimotor learning in the auditory cortex
Yves Boubenec  |  Neuro team, Laboratoire des Systèmes Perceptifs (LSP, CNRS UMR 8248)
Département d’Études Cognitives, École Normale Supérieure – PSL, Paris
Scientific Context
A central problem in systems neuroscience is understanding how the brain learns to predict the sensory consequences of its own actions and, conversely, how it transforms sensory goals into appropriate motor commands. These two computations, commonly formalized as the forward and inverse problems of sensorimotor control, are thought to be implemented by internal models distributed across interconnected sensory and motor areas (Kawato, 1999). While considerable progress has been made in the visuomotor domain, auditory-motor integration remains comparatively poorly understood, a gap that is particularly consequential given its role in speech, vocal learning, and musical performance.
The concept of corollary discharge, a copy of the motor command broadcast to sensory regions in advance of movement, provides the theoretical backbone for understanding how the brain anticipates the acoustic consequences of self-generated motion. Anatomical and physiological evidence in rodents and primates has established that premotor and secondary motor cortices project directly onto primary auditory cortex (A1), and that these inputs suppress auditory responses during movement, effectively cancelling predictable reafferent sounds (Schneider et al., 2014; Schneider & Mooney, 2018). Crucially, this suppression is not a fixed, hardwired reflex: it is learned and acoustically specific. Using a paradigm in which mice were trained to generate sounds by running on a treadmill, Schneider et al. (2018) showed that A1 develops a learned filter that selectively attenuates the expected acoustic consequences of movement, and that this filter is abolished when the sound-movement contingency is disrupted. This work established that A1 is not merely a passive recipient of motor signals but an active participant in learning to predict self-generated sounds.
Despite this progress, three fundamental questions remain unresolved. First, how are sensorimotor computations distributed across the auditory cortical hierarchy? Primary and non-primary auditory fields are anatomically and functionally distinct (Landemard et al., 2021), and there is strong theoretical motivation, grounded in predictive coding frameworks, to expect that prediction errors and motor anticipatory signals are differently represented across hierarchical levels (Friston, 2005; Rao & Ballard, 1999). Whether motor predictions propagate top-down from premotor cortex preferentially through higher auditory fields, or whether A1 itself is the primary locus of sensorimotor integration, is unknown. Second, how does the brain acquire, store, and switch between multiple distinct sensorimotor mappings? The nervous system must manage a repertoire of internal models and retrieve the appropriate one depending on context (Kawato, 1999). The neural substrates of this capacity in the auditory system are entirely unexplored. Third, how are auditory targets converted into motor commands during voluntary acoustic production? While forward models predict the sensory consequences of a given movement, the inverse problem requires specifying what movement to execute to achieve a desired acoustic outcome, a computation critical to speech and music production whose cortical implementation remains poorly characterized.
These questions are directly addressable in the ferret, a gyrencephalic carnivore with a well-characterized auditory cortical hierarchy including primary (A1) and secondary areas (AAF, PSF, ADF), and a premotor cortex accessible to chronic multi-electrode recording (Fritz et al., 2010; Landemard et al., 2021). The ferret exhibits robust cortical plasticity during auditory task learning, and we have recently developed a closed-loop paradigm in freely moving ferrets that creates an arbitrary and controllable mapping between head position and acoustic frequency, providing the first platform to study the neural correlates of auditory-motor learning at the single-neuron level across the full learning trajectory from naïve to expert.
Preliminary Data
Our laboratory has developed a closed-loop system in which the online-tracked azimuthal snout position of a head-restrained ferret is continuously converted into the frequency of a note played through a speaker (Fig. 1A, B). This creates a tight, arbitrary coupling between self-generated movement and auditory feedback. We interleave this closed-loop condition with an open-loop playback condition, in which the exact sound sequence previously generated by the animal is replayed, with identical acoustics but no sensorimotor contingency. Animals are chronically implanted with 32-channel floating microelectrode arrays in A1, and recordings from the same units are compared across the two conditions, allowing us to isolate neural correlates of sensorimotor contingency while holding acoustics constant.
After several weeks of continuous closed-loop exposure, we observe four classes of A1 neuronal changes. First, a preparatory signal emerges prior to movement onset, exclusively in the closed-loop condition (Fig. 1C). This anticipatory activity rises above baseline approximately 200–300 ms before the first movement following a stationary period (defined by extended flat segments of the head position trace, shown as insets in Fig. 1C), and is absent in both open-loop (sound alone) and passive movement conditions. Importantly, this signal builds progressively with exposure: it is stronger in animals with three weeks of training (expert) than two weeks (intermediate) or one week (beginner), demonstrating that it is a correlate of sensorimotor learning rather than a fixed property of A1. Second, sustained A1 activity is systematically modulated by head speed (Fig. 1D), and population-level decoding of head speed from A1 activity is significantly more accurate in the closed-loop than open-loop condition (Fig. 1E), demonstrating that A1 encodes kinematic parameters of self-generated movement as a consequence of the learned contingency. Third, we observe a sharpening of frequency representations in A1 with learning (data not shown), suggesting that sensorimotor experience sculpts sensory tuning. Fourth, simultaneous recordings from A1 and premotor cortex (PMC) reveal that single-trial co-fluctuations of activity between the two areas (quantified as noise correlations) are significantly stronger in the closed-loop than open-loop condition, and increase progressively with learning (Fig. 1F), consistent with the progressive consolidation of premotor-to-auditory connectivity underlying the learned predictions.
Our working hypothesis is that sensorimotor learning strengthens correlated activity between PMC neurons encoding movement parameters and A1 neurons encoding the corresponding acoustic frequencies, establishing a learned forward model instantiated in the synaptic architecture of the A1-PMC circuit. The anticipatory signal in A1 reflects the deployment of this learned model: as movement is initiated, the motor command broadcasts a predictive signal to A1 that pre-activates the expected acoustic representation.
Scientific Goals
Goal 1: Mapping the distribution of sensorimotor signals across the auditory cortical hierarchy. 
Our current data are restricted to A1. We will determine whether the anticipatory motor signal, the kinematic encoding, and the strengthened A1-PMC correlations are specific to A1 or shared across the auditory hierarchy, and whether directed connectivity analyses reveal a top-down propagation pattern from PMC to auditory areas or a bottom-up emergence pattern. This will allow us to test whether sensorimotor predictions are generated at the level of primary cortex or inherited from hierarchically higher areas.
Goal 2: Characterizing the storage and retrieval of multiple sensorimotor mappings. 
Animals will be trained on two or more distinct position-to-frequency mappings, alternated either in blocks or interleaved. We will ask whether A1 and PMC population representations maintain latent encodings of multiple mappings, how rapidly the system can switch between them, and whether retrieval of a previously learned mapping is associated with a different neural signature than de novo learning of a new one. This will test whether the auditory cortex acts as a multi-model repository and illuminate the computational principles governing the storage of sensorimotor knowledge.

Goal 3: Characterizing inverse model computation during goal-directed acoustic production. 
In a new behavioral variant, animals will be trained to reach target frequencies on demand for reward, requiring them to solve the inverse problem of computing what head position produces a desired acoustic outcome. Comparing neural activity during this goal-directed production with the passive closed-loop condition will reveal whether A1 encodes motor goals in a sensory coordinate frame, as would be expected if it participates in inverse model computation, or whether its role is restricted to forward prediction.

Experimental Methods
Behavioral paradigm. 
All experiments use freely moving ferrets in a head-fixed configuration with chronic implants. The closed-loop system converts online-tracked azimuthal snout position into acoustic frequency in real time using a custom software pipeline. Alternating open-loop playback sessions interleaved within recording sessions allow within-session comparison of closed-loop versus open-loop conditions in the same neurons. For Goal 2, two mappings with reversed or orthogonal position-to-frequency assignments will be alternated in blocks of variable length to probe switching dynamics and latent representation. For Goal 3, a target-frequency task will be implemented using a reward delivery system triggered when the animal holds a target frequency band for a criterion duration.
Electrophysiological recordings. 
Single- and multi-unit recordings in A1 are currently performed using 32-channel tungsten floating microelectrode arrays (MicroProbes), which provide stable chronic recordings from the same neurons over several months. This preparation has been validated in 5 animals and 7 hemispheres in the laboratory. To address Goals 1 and 3, which require simultaneous multi-area recordings with large populations, we will complement the arrays with Neuropixels 2.0 probes (IMEC), which deliver 50–100 simultaneously isolated single units per shank. Our team has successfully implanted Neuropixels probes in 2 animals and validated the compatibility of this preparation with the chronic closed-loop paradigm. The multi-area recording configuration will combine a fixed 32-channel array targeting secondary auditory cortex (AAF or PSF) with a Neuropixels probe targeting A1, together with a second array in PMC, a configuration that has been validated in our laboratory as mechanically and biologically compatible.
Data analysis. 
Neural population dynamics will be analyzed using dimensionality reduction (PCA, GPFA), population decoding (linear and nonlinear regression of kinematic variables from population activity), and directed connectivity analyses (Granger causality, partial directed coherence on local field potentials and spike trains). For Goal 2, we will apply demixed PCA and latent factor models to identify subspaces corresponding to each mapping and to characterize their overlap and orthogonality. For Goal 3, encoding models comparing tuning to acoustic targets versus to motor commands will determine the coordinate frame of A1 representations during goal-directed production.
